# How Orders Execute

Order types are usually explained as a list of names. The names are the easy part. What decides
whether a strategy behaves as intended is a smaller set of mechanics underneath them.

---

## Trigger and fill are two different events

This is the distinction that explains most surprises.

A **trigger** is a condition being met — price reached your stop level. A **fill** is a trade
actually happening. Between the two, price keeps moving.

| Order | Triggers when | Fills at | Can it fail to fill? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market | Immediately | Best available price | No |
| Limit | Immediately | Your price **or better** | Yes — if price never reaches it |
| Stop Market | Price hits the stop | Best available price at that moment | No |
| Stop Limit | Price hits the stop | Your limit price or better | **Yes** |

So a **Stop Market** loss order always closes you, but not necessarily at the number you typed —
in a fast move the fill can be materially worse. That gap is slippage, and it is the price of
certainty.

A **Stop Limit** protects you from a bad fill and, in exchange, can leave you in the position
entirely. If price gaps straight through your limit, nothing fills and the loss keeps running.

:::warning[The trade-off has no free side]
A market stop guarantees you are out at an uncertain price. A limit stop guarantees the price and
not the exit. Choosing "limit" for a stop loss because it sounds safer is the more dangerous of
the two options in exactly the conditions a stop exists for.
:::

## Which price triggers it

A trigger has to be measured against *some* price, and there is more than one.

| Price type | What it is | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Mark price** | A smoothed reference price, the default | Resistant to brief wicks and thin-book spikes |
| **Last price** | The most recent actual trade | Reacts to every print, including outliers |
| **Index price** | An aggregate across external venues | Least affected by any single venue |

Mark price is the default because it is the conservative choice: a single wick on thin liquidity
should not close a position that the wider market never actually moved against. Selecting last
price makes stops fire more readily — sometimes wanted, more often not.

## Sizing: three ways to say "how much"

| Form | Example | Behaviour as the account grows |
|---|---|---|
| Percentage of balance | `2%` | Position size grows with the account — it compounds |
| USD value | `$300` | Constant risk per trade regardless of balance |
| Token amount | `0.001 BTC` | Constant token exposure; USD risk moves with price |

None is correct in general. Percentage sizing compounds gains and drawdowns alike; fixed USD
sizing keeps a losing streak from shrinking your position size just as a strategy recovers.

## The take-profit ladder

Up to **10** take-profit levels can be attached to one position. Each is an independent order
against a slice of the position.

Take a long from $45,000:

| Level | Price | Slice |
|---|---|---|
| TP1 | $48,000 | 30% |
| TP2 | $50,000 | 40% |
| TP3 | $52,000 | 30% |

Price reaching $48,000 closes 30% and leaves the rest running. If it then reverses, TP2 and TP3
stay open — they are not cancelled, and they are not "missed". They simply have not triggered.
Slices are set in the terminal as a token amount, a USD value or a percentage. Over the webhook
API they must be **token amounts**, and their total must not exceed the order quantity — see
[JSON Format](/docs/developer/json-format#multiple-take-profits).

**A ladder changes the shape of your outcome, not your edge.** It converts one binary result into
a distribution: you give up some of the best case in exchange for banking part of the move
earlier. That is a preference, not an improvement.

## Moving the stop as profit accrues

Two mechanisms trail a stop, and they respond to different things.

**Trailing stop** follows *price*. It ratchets up behind the peak and never moves back down.
Distance is fixed or a percentage, and trailing starts at an activation price.

**SLX (Stop Loss X)** follows *your take-profit events*, which is what makes it distinctive:

- `breakeven` — once the activation TP fills, the stop moves to entry. The trade can no longer
  lose money.
- `follow_tp` — after each TP fills, the stop moves up to the previous TP level, locking in each
  step of the ladder as it completes.

SLX also has a `callback_rate` mode that trails by a percentage from the peak, which is the same
idea as a trailing stop expressed in the automation payload.

## Isolated and cross margin

| Mode | Margin at risk | Liquidation |
|---|---|---|
| **Isolated** | Only what you allocated to that position | That position only; the rest of the balance survives |
| **Cross** | The whole slot balance backs every position | Can take the account down with it |

Cross margin gives a position more room before liquidation, by putting everything else behind it.
Isolated caps the damage at a known number. For automated strategies — where a bad signal can
arrive while you are asleep — isolated is the mode that lets you state your worst case in advance.
